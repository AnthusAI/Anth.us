Turn detection is the subtle art of knowing when a user has finished speaking. In a voice bot, getting this wrong leads to two painful failure modes:

1.  **The Interruption**: The bot cuts you off while you're pausing to check your credit card number.
2.  **The Awkward Silence**: You finish speaking, and the bot stares blankly for three seconds before replying.

Most systems rely on a standard Voice Activity Detector (VAD) or a general-purpose language model. But we wanted to answer a specific question: **"If we keep the architecture and latency target fixed (MobileBERT, less than 50ms) and only change the training corpus from 'general conversations' to 'call-center transcripts', how much accuracy do we gain?"**

The answer was stark: we improved accuracy from **87.5% to 100%** on the target domain. But getting there was an adventure in model architecture debugging.

## The Experiment Design

We trained four different model variants to test how granular we needed to be. All models were based on **MobileBERT** (chosen for its speed on CPU), but trained on different data slices:

1.  **Model_General**: Trained on PersonaChat and TURNS-2K (approx. 20k sentences of casual conversation).
2.  **Model_Domain**: Trained on the AIxBlock Call Center dataset (approx. 73k sentences of real support calls).
3.  **Model_Agent**: Trained only on the *agent* side of the call center conversations.
4.  **Model_Customer**: Trained only on the *customer* side.

To prevent the model from simply memorizing sentences, we enforced a **one-shot rule**: every unique sentence appeared exactly once in the training set, either as a complete utterance or a randomly truncated incomplete version.

## Findings: The "Generalization" Myth

We expected the general model to fail hard on domain-specific jargon. Surprisingly, it didn't.

*   **Model_General** achieved **87.5% accuracy** on call center data.
*   This was only a **1.1% drop** from its performance on its own general test set (88.6%).

This suggests that MobileBERT's pre-training provides a very robust baseline for understanding sentence structure. It knows that "My name is..." is incomplete, regardless of context.

However, **87.5% isn't good enough for a voice bot.** That 12.5% error rate means 1 in 8 turns will be mishandled—either an interruption or a lag.

## The Power of Specialization

When we switched to **Model_Domain**, accuracy on the call center test set jumped to **100%**.

It turns out that "completeness" in a call center context is highly pattern-based. Customers use specific phrasings ("I'm calling about...", "My account number is...") that distinctively signal whether they are done. By training on the exact domain, the model learned these patterns perfectly.

Even more interestingly, the **Model_Agent** and **Model_Customer** variants *also* achieved 100% accuracy on their respective test sets. This suggests that we could potentially deploy ultra-specialized models for each side of the conversation if we really wanted to squeeze out every drop of performance.

## The Technical "Gotcha": MobileBERT's Missing Layer

The most frustrating part of this adventure wasn't the data—it was the model itself.

When we first tried to fine-tune `google/mobilebert-uncased` using the standard HuggingFace `MobileBERTForSequenceClassification` class, the training failed completely. Loss stagnated around 0.7 (random guessing), and accuracy refused to budge.

After digging into the architecture, we found the culprit: **MobileBERT lacks the pre-classifier normalization layer found in standard BERT.**

In standard BERT, the pooler output goes through a dense layer and a `Tanh` activation before hitting the classifier. In the default MobileBERT implementation, the raw hidden states were being fed directly into the classifier. This caused gradients to explode or vanish during fine-tuning.

We had to patch the model architecture to inject a `LayerNorm` before the final classification head:

```python
# The critical fix for MobileBERT fine-tuning
self.layer_norm = nn.LayerNorm(config.hidden_size)
self.dropout = nn.Dropout(0.1)
self.classifier = nn.Linear(config.hidden_size, config.num_labels)
```

With this single change, the training dynamics normalized instantly, and the model converged rapidly.

## Why Turn Detection Matters

Turn detection is a binary decision ("Complete" vs. "Incomplete"), but the cues that matter depend heavily on context. Casual chat looks nothing like a call center script. We wanted to measure how much accuracy we leave on the table when we *don't* train on the right domain.

Imagine a support bot that says "Thanks for calling!" while a caller is still spelling their account number, or a three-second silence after "My account number is 12345." Both are turn-detection failures:

* **False positives** → interrupting humans mid-sentence.
* **False negatives** → awkward silence after they’re done.

## Key Findings Summary

1.  **Domain-specific training works**: We saw a 12.5 percentage point improvement on call center data (87.5% → 100%).
2.  **General models have decent cross-domain transfer**: Only a 1.1pp drop when moving from general to call center (88.6% → 87.5%). MobileBERT's pretraining provides a good baseline generalization.
3.  **The trade-off is operational, not technical**: "Loss of generalization" is irrelevant (you don't use domain models out-of-domain). The real cost is the engineering work to run several models.
4.  **MobileBERT needs LayerNorm for fine-tuning**: This was a critical bug fix. Without normalization, gradient explosion occurred. With it, we achieved excellent results.
5.  **Channel-specific models also achieve 100%**: Both Agent and Customer models hit perfect accuracy, suggesting even finer-grained specialization could work, though at higher operational cost.

## Future Directions

This experiment raised several interesting questions for future work:

*   **Data Efficiency**: We used ~73K call center examples. Could we get 95%+ accuracy with just 10K or 1K examples?
*   **Hybrid Models**: Could we use multi-task learning or adapter layers to get the best of both worlds without managing multiple full models?
*   **Other Domains**: Would we see similar gains in healthcare (doctor-patient) or technical support contexts?
*   **Production Optimization**: Next steps would include ONNX export for faster inference, INT8 quantization, and potentially adding acoustic features (pitch, prosody) for ambiguous cases like "Okay" or "Yes".

## The Operational Trade-off

If domain-specific training is so effective, why doesn't everyone do it?

The trade-off isn't technical; it's operational. A general model is easy to deploy: one API, one endpoint, one maintenance cycle. Domain-specific models require you to manage a fleet of models—one for banking, one for healthcare, one for retail.

But our experiment proves the value is there. For high-volume enterprise voice bots where customer experience is paramount, that 12.5 percentage point improvement is the difference between a "robotic" IVR and a fluid, human-like conversation.
