You're searching for how to fix a slow Node.js API. The first five Google results look promising—detailed tutorials, code samples, performance benchmarks. You click the first link.

Generic advice. Hallucinated function names. Code samples that don't compile. The second link? Same thing. The third? Also AI-generated, but this time it's literally the same article with different synonyms swapped in ("optimize" → "enhance," "performance" → "efficiency").

By the tenth result, you realize: **every single page is AI slop**. The internet didn't run out of information about Node.js performance. The real information got buried under an avalanche of synthetic mediocrity.

This is the Content Paradox.

And this article is AI slop. A model wrote the first draft. I edited it, checked the citations, and cut a few hundred words of outline-shaped filler. That is not a joke and it is not a humblebrag. If cheaper writing floods the internet with fluent mediocrity, a site like this is part of the flood. The only difference, if there is one, is whether a human with a point of view was willing to cut, check, and sign it.

## The Pattern is Everywhere

This isn't isolated to technical documentation. CNET published AI finance explainers that needed a pile of corrections. Sports Illustrated published product copy under fake author profiles with AI-generated headshots; its publisher denied that the copy itself was generated by AI. LinkedIn fills with "thought leadership" farmed for engagement at industrial scale.

Search has the same problem. HouseFresh, a site that actually tests air purifiers, lost most of its Google traffic to big-media SEO pages and, later, to AI Overviews that summarize the work and keep the click.

The counterintuitive question: **Why did making content easier to create make information harder to find?**

## The Coal Paradox

In 1865, British economist William Stanley Jevons noticed something strange about coal.<Citation
data={{
    type: "book",
    title: "The Coal Question: An Inquiry Concerning the Progress of the Nation, and the Probable Exhaustion of Our Coal-Mines",
    author: "William Stanley Jevons",
    publisher: "Macmillan and Co.",
    issued: { "date-parts": [[1865]] },
  }}
/>

James Watt's more efficient steam engine let industry do the same work with less coal. The logical assumption was that coal consumption would fall. Instead, it exploded.

Why? <mark>Because efficiency does not guarantee conservation. By making something cheaper to use, it can unlock much more demand.</mark>

The efficiency **enabled new uses**. Steam engines became viable for factories, trains, ships, and pumps that never would have used them before. Demand grew faster than efficiency could save fuel.

This became **Jevons Paradox**: the counterintuitive case in which efficiency increases total resource consumption instead of reducing it. Economists now treat it as the strongest form of a broader phenomenon called the rebound effect.

The pattern repeats. Wider highways attract more drivers. Free, instant email produces spam. The printing press gave us more of everything, including garbage, while publishers, editors, and libraries struggled to filter it.

Now AI is doing the same thing to content.

## The AI Content Mechanism

AI writing tools dropped production costs by orders of magnitude. Hours became seconds. Skill became a prompt. A content farm can publish hundreds of articles where it once published ten, and people who were never writers—or experts—can generate "expertise" at machine scale.

The incentives did not improve with the tools. SEO spam still works. Affiliate pages still pay. Farms still earn on pageviews, not value. When generation is nearly free, volume wins.

<mark>
  AI makes content easier to produce, but it does not make judgment cheaper.
</mark>

A researched piece still takes expertise, taste, and time. "10 Tips for Node.js Performance" takes thirty seconds and a template. AI democratizes production. Excellence still needs an editor.

We train models on the internet. If future training sets become dominated by model output, each generation trains on a photocopy of a photocopy.

Researchers call this model collapse.<Citation

/> The informal name is "Habsburg AI." Either way: tails of the distribution disappear, and the output gets more generic.

## The Information Commons

Jevons points toward a deeper problem: **the tragedy of the commons**.

The internet is a shared information resource. Everyone benefits when it is trustworthy, but the actors who pollute it can still get paid. Generators capture the upside from ads, affiliates, and rankings. Everyone else absorbs worse search and wasted attention.

Garrett Hardin described the dynamic in 1968: when a resource is shared and costs are externalized, rational actors will overuse it.<Citation

/> We don't have a scarcity of content. We have a scarcity of curation.

That is the same failure mode as [Cybernetic Development](/cybernetic-development): optimize for generation speed without a governor, and you get slop at scale, whether the output is brittle software or synthetic essays.

## We've Solved This Before

The good news: <mark>we have seen this pattern before, and the response is not to smash the press.</mark>

Email produced filters and sender reputation. Print produced editors, publishers, libraries, and criticism. The response is not to reject efficiency. It is to **govern the system around it**.

For AI content, that means search engines that deindex slop and reward reputation over time. It means communities that add friction on purpose: Stack Overflow banned generative-AI posts because plausible wrong answers swamped volunteer review. It means business models that do not depend entirely on pageviews: subscriptions, patronage, newsletters.

For people writing: make things that are hard to fake—lived detail, primary sources, a voice, a record. Generic how-tos automate. Judgment does not.

For people reading: get pickier. Follow humans. Pay the ones worth following. Treat generic phrasing and unsourced specifics as a warning.

## Conclusion: Efficiency Without Wisdom

The question is not whether to use AI for content. The tools are too useful, and the efficiency is real. The question is: **How do we govern generation so the efficiency does not become pollution?**

Jevons Paradox says **efficiency is not a solution. It is a multiplier.** On coal: industry and soot. On writing: useful knowledge, and slop.

AI did not break the internet. It exposed incentives that were already broken: clicks over quality, volume over value.

The winners will not be whoever generates the most. They will be whoever knows when to generate, when to curate, and when to shut up.

<mark>
  That takes judgment—the kind that comes from understanding both the tools and
  the mess they make.
</mark>

Cheaper does not mean better. Sometimes it just means more. Including this.
