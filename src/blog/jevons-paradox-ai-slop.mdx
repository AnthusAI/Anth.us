You're searching for how to fix a slow Node.js API. The first five Google results look promising—detailed tutorials, code samples, performance benchmarks. You click the first link.

Generic advice. Hallucinated function names. Code samples that don't compile. The second link? Same thing. The third? Also AI-generated, but this time it's literally the same article with different synonyms swapped in ("optimize" → "enhance," "performance" → "efficiency").

By the tenth result, you realize: **every single page is AI slop**. The internet didn't run out of information about Node.js performance. The real information got buried under an avalanche of synthetic mediocrity.

This is the Content Paradox.

And this article is AI slop. A model wrote the first draft. I edited it, checked the citations, and cut a few hundred words of outline-shaped filler. That is not a joke and it is not a humblebrag. If cheaper writing floods the internet with fluent mediocrity, a site like this is part of the flood. The only difference, if there is one, is whether a human with a point of view was willing to cut, check, and sign it.

## The Pattern is Everywhere

This isn't isolated to technical documentation.

**Amazon**: verified reviews buried under generated fakes, listings that invent features.

**LinkedIn**: "thought leadership" that reads like a prompt with the temperature turned down, farmed for engagement at industrial scale.

**News**: CNET published AI finance explainers that needed a pile of corrections. Sports Illustrated ran product copy under fake author profiles with AI headshots. Local news deserts filled with farms that ship hundreds of articles a day.

**Academia**: papers that still contain "As a large language model." Peer review costs rising faster than journals can adapt.

**Search**: HouseFresh, a site that actually tests air purifiers, lost most of its Google traffic to big-media SEO pages and, later, to AI Overviews that summarize the work and keep the click.

The counterintuitive question: **Why did making content easier to create make information harder to find?**

## The Coal Paradox

In 1865, British economist William Stanley Jevons noticed something strange about coal.<Citation

/>

James Watt's improved steam engine had made coal use dramatically more efficient. The logical assumption was that this would reduce coal consumption—after all, you could now do the same work with less fuel.

The opposite happened. Coal consumption exploded.

Why? <mark>Because when you make something cheaper, you don't get better use of it—you get more of it.</mark>

The improved efficiency didn't reduce demand; it **enabled new uses**. Steam engines became viable for factories, trains, ships, and pumps that never would have used them before. Total consumption rose far past what the efficiency gains had saved.

This became **Jevons Paradox** (or the rebound effect): improvements in resource efficiency tend to increase—not decrease—total resource consumption.

The pattern has repeated:

- **Highways**: widen a road to ease congestion and you attract more drivers. Traffic engineers call it induced demand.
- **Email**: free, instant messages did not produce fewer, more thoughtful notes. They produced spam.
- **Printing press**: Gutenberg did not give us less print. He gave us more of everything, including garbage. Filtering institutions (publishers, editors, peer review, libraries) took centuries to catch up.

Now AI is doing the same thing to content.

## The AI Content Mechanism

AI writing tools dropped the cost of content by orders of magnitude. Hours became seconds. Skill became a prompt.

Five mechanisms:

### 1. Collapsed production costs

A farm that shipped 10 articles a day can ship 1,000. A solo SEO operator who ran 5 sites can run 500.

### 2. Induced demand

People who were never writers, and never experts, can now generate "writing" and "expertise" at machine scale. The market floods with new suppliers.

### 3. Perverse incentives

SEO spam still works. Affiliate pages still pay. Farms still earn on pageviews, not value. When generation is nearly free, volume wins.

### 4. The quality paradox

<mark>AI makes it easier to create bad content, not good content.</mark> A researched piece still takes expertise, judgment, and time. "10 Tips for Node.js Performance" takes thirty seconds and a template. AI democratizes mediocrity. It does little for excellence.

### 5. The feedback loop

We train models on the internet. If the internet is mostly model output, each generation trains on a photocopy of a photocopy.

Researchers call this model collapse.<Citation

## The Tragedy of the Information Commons

Jevons points at a deeper problem: **the tragedy of the commons**.

The internet is a shared information resource. Everyone benefits when it is trustworthy. Each actor is paid to pollute it.

The spammer publishes 10,000 articles for pennies and skims ad money. Their cost is trivial. The cost to everyone else—worse search, wasted attention—is enormous, and externalized. Platforms get paid for the pageviews. Searchers have no way to tax the pollution.

Garrett Hardin described the dynamic in 1968: when a resource is shared and costs are externalized, rational actors will overuse it.<Citation

## AI Slop and the Death of Discovery

The term **AI slop** showed up around 2023–2024 on Hacker News and Reddit: low-quality generated content that clogs platforms and search. Related to spam, but not the same. Spam is unwanted commercial messages. Slop is unwanted synthetic content.

The economics are simple:

- **Cost to create**: about $0.001 per article
- **Cost to distribute**: about $0
- **Revenue**: $0.01–$5 per thousand pageviews
- **Human attention**: priceless, and externalized

A negative-sum game. Generators extract a little (ads, affiliates, rankings). The collective cost—time, trust, a polluted commons—is much larger.

Herbert Simon said it in 1971: a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.<Citation

That is the same failure mode as [Cybernetic Development](/cybernetic-development): optimize for generation speed without a governor, and you get slop at scale, whether the output is brittle software or synthetic essays.

## The Dead Internet Theory

The "Dead Internet Theory" claims the internet died around 2016 and is now mostly bots talking to bots. It is not literally true. It is a decent description of the *vibe*. Search, scroll, browse, and you are increasingly talking to wallpaper.

<mark>Tools meant to help us communicate made communication worse by enabling an unprecedented volume of low-quality content.</mark>

Peter Howson made the energy version of this argument in *The Conversation*: if DeepSeek really did make training cheaper, Jevons says total AI use—and total environmental cost—goes up, not down.<Citation

## We've Solved This Before

The good news: <mark>this is a pattern we have seen, and we know the response is not to smash the press.</mark>

Printing produced publishers, peer review, libraries, copyright, and criticism. Email produced Bayesian filters, sender reputation, and CAN-SPAM. Induced highway demand produced transit, congestion pricing, and land use that shortens trips.

The move is never to make the efficient thing less efficient. It is to **govern the system around it**.

For AI content that means search engines that deindex slop and reward reputation over time (an arms race, but the only game). It means communities that add friction on purpose: Stack Overflow banned generative-AI posts because plausible wrong answers swamp volunteer review. Many Reddit communities did the same; the company never did, sitewide. It means money that does not come from pageviews: subscriptions, patronage, newsletters.

For people writing: make things that are hard to fake. Lived detail, primary sources, a voice, a record. Generic how-tos automate. Judgment does not.

For people reading: get pickier. Follow humans. Pay the ones worth following. Assume generic phrasing and unsourced specifics are a warning, not a style.

Disclosure labels and liability rules may help. They will not substitute for a market that pays for trust instead of volume.

## The Right Question

Not: **"Should we use AI for content?"** The tools are too useful. The efficiency is real.

The question is: **"How do we govern generation so the efficiency does not become pollution?"**

Jevons again: efficiency raises total consumption. Ungoverned systems optimize for volume. Individual rationality (cheap generation) produces collective stupidity (a ruined commons).

The winners will not be whoever generates the most. They will be whoever knows when to generate, when to curate, and when to shut up.

<mark>That takes judgment—the kind that comes from understanding both the tools and the mess they make.</mark>

## Conclusion: Efficiency Without Wisdom

Jevons Paradox says **efficiency is not a solution. It is a multiplier.** It amplifies whatever system you drop it into.

On coal: industry and soot. On highways: mobility and sprawl. On writing: useful knowledge, and this.

<mark>Technology does not solve human problems by itself. It exposes them.</mark>

AI did not break the internet. It showed that the incentives were already broken: clicks over quality, volume over value.

The work is to build a system that pays for quality, punishes slop, and treats human attention as scarce. We did versions of that for spam and for print. We can do it for generated text, but only if we stop treating "more, faster" as the goal.

Cheaper does not mean better. Sometimes it just means more. Including this.
